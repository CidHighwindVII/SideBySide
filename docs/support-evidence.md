# Support-first redesign — evidence and limits

The dates the user enters can inform an *estimated next period*, but do not
establish another person's mood, energy, willingness or needs. This release
replaces phase-based social traffic with an ask-first catalog. A saved support
preference is user-entered, not verified partner consent. Ratings measure
whether **the user's action** felt useful to the user, not how the partner felt.

Evidence informing the choice, not a claim that this app is clinically proven:

- Henry et al. (2024), a prospective cohort of 53 women, found meaningful
  within-person variability in phase lengths, including luteal lengths:
  https://doi.org/10.1093/humrep/deae215
- Johnson et al. (2018) found cycle-length-only calendar methods could not
  accurately identify the day of ovulation. Their result is not an accuracy
  estimate of SideBySide: https://doi.org/10.1080/03007995.2018.1475348
- Ussher and Perz (2017) found differences in behavioural coping and reported
  partner understanding in a trial of couple CBT for premenstrual disorders.
  This does **not** establish that app suggestions have a treatment effect:
  https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0175068
- Mohan and Jenkins (2025) interviewed 25 app users about privacy, access to
  their data and usable predictions: https://doi.org/10.1093/oodh/oqaf011
- Bucher et al. (2025) evaluated 14 apps for education and inclusiveness;
  this was an app audit, not a clinical trial:
  https://doi.org/10.1186/s12905-025-03812-1

Before attributing impact, test acceptability with both users and people
whose dates might be entered. Check whether the first useful action, logging
a date, explaining uncertainty and deleting a preference can be done easily;
do not infer partner benefit from action feedback alone.
